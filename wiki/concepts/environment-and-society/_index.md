# Environment and society

This section covers upstream environments and systems that shape health, behavior, mobility, and technological change. [[environmental-pollution-and-health]] and [[microplastics-exposure-and-measurement]] establish exposure-to-dose-to-outcome reasoning; [[indoor-air-quality]] applies it to the built envelope where most exposure hours accrue — accumulation versus dilution, source control before hardware, and the thin intervention-trial base. [[safe-streets-and-pedestrian-risk]], [[transit-capacity-and-service-design]], and [[intercity-travel-generalized-cost]] apply systems reasoning to the built environment. The information chapters distinguish useful communication from validated output: [[human-centered-ai-and-learning]] addresses tutoring, spatial intelligence, embodiment, and governance; [[ai-assisted-science-communication]] explains probabilistic generation, verification asymmetry, provenance, automation bias, and cognitive-offloading uncertainty; and [[health-misinformation-and-media-incentives]] maps how novelty, authority, correction placement, and affiliate revenue can turn weak claims into high-reach health advice. [[public-trust-in-longevity-science]] and [[replication-and-research-incentives]] turn the same systems reasoning on research itself: the first treats public legitimacy as a measurable input that gates funding and regulation, the second explains why patent protection, novelty preference, and publication bias leave consequential claims unchecked. [[open-data-and-research-infrastructure]] completes that argument at the level of evidence itself: a published article is an interpretation of data rather than the data, and it sets out what independent access adds, why annotation decays sharply with time since the experiment, and why the fix for a reward system denominated entirely in journal articles is to raise the rewards for the neglected work rather than to mandate the effort.
