# Placebo effect

The measurable improvement produced by expectation, ritual, attention,
and context rather than by a molecule's pharmacology. Genuinely powerful
for subjective outcomes — pain is the cleanest example, because pain is
shaped by attention, expectation, threat perception, sleep, mood, and
context (Peter Attia MD — "403 ‒ Peptides: separating scientific promise
from marketing hype", 2026-08-10,
[link](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Km_01CLEkYU)).

## Amplifiers

Features that enlarge the placebo response: a powerful narrative
("regenerative molecule that succeeds where conventional medicine
failed"), social media testimonials, authority from a clinician or
clinic, real cost, and subcutaneous injection — which "feels more
official." Gray-market [[peptides]] have nearly all of these (Peter Attia
MD — "403 ‒ Peptides: separating scientific promise from marketing
hype", 2026-08-10, [link](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Km_01CLEkYU)).

## RCTs answer the attribution question

Randomized controlled trials exist to say how much improvement belongs to
the molecule after accounting for story, ritual, attention, and
expectation. Even for drugs that clearly work: in the STEP 1 trial,
semaglutide produced far more weight loss than placebo — but the placebo
group, believing they might be on a weight-loss drug, still lost weight.
Trial context, lifestyle support, adherence, and follow-up move outcomes
on their own (Peter Attia MD — "403 ‒ Peptides: separating scientific
promise from marketing hype", 2026-08-10,
[link](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Km_01CLEkYU)). For compounds like
[[bpc-157]], where the story is powerful and controlled human data are
thin, anecdotes become easy to over-interpret (Peter Attia MD — "403 ‒
Peptides: separating scientific promise from marketing hype", 2026-08-10,
[link](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Km_01CLEkYU)).
