# Biological age biomarkers

Biological-age biomarkers compress molecular or clinical measurements into an estimate of accumulated state, pace of change, or mortality risk. These are different targets. DunedinPACE, for example, is calibrated like a speedometer around 1.0 biological year per chronological year; an age clock instead estimates a state relative to reference ages. ## Interpretation and evidence

Clocks are usefully sorted by what they were trained to predict, because the training target determines what a reading can mean. **First-generation** clocks were trained on chronological age, largely because chronological age is the label most abundantly available. They became highly accurate — around two years of average error — which creates a paradox worth stating plainly: a perfect chronological-age predictor would convey nothing that a passport does not. The informative quantity in a first-generation clock is therefore the *prediction error*, the variance around the predicted age, with the hope being that a person predicted older than their calendar age is aging faster than average. That association with mortality and disease exists but is weaker than one would want, which is precisely what motivated the next step. **Second-generation** clocks were trained on a biological readout such as mortality directly; the training data are far scarcer, but the resulting scores track disease and mortality substantially better. A subsequent step trains across biological boundaries rather than outcomes: pan-tissue clocks predict age across many tissues from one model, and a pan-mammalian clock tracks biological age across a variety of mammal species, which is remarkable simply for working at all. (@TheSheekeyScienceShow (The Sheekey Science Show) — "How Randomness Drives Aging - DNA Repair, Clocks & Rejuvenation (David Meyer)", 2025-07-04, [link](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Buj07nWt7o0))

That progression also explains why clocks proliferate rather than converge. A pan-tissue clock does not perform equally well on every tissue, partly because training data were unevenly available across tissues, so tissue-specific clocks were built where more data existed; the same pressure applies to rare tissues, rare species, and unusual datasets, each of which invites a refined clock of its own. Clock proliferation is thus a consequence of data availability and target choice, not evidence of accumulating knowledge about aging, and it is a reason to expect that two clocks disagreeing about the same sample may both be behaving as designed. (@TheSheekeyScienceShow (The Sheekey Science Show) — "How Randomness Drives Aging - DNA Repair, Clocks & Rejuvenation (David Meyer)", 2025-07-04, [link](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Buj07nWt7o0))

DNA methylation clocks select patterns across hundreds of CpG sites, but different clocks answer different questions. First-generation clocks were optimized to predict chronological age; PhenoAge and GrimAge incorporate methylation proxies for clinical phenotypes, inflammatory proteins, and smoking exposure; DunedinPACE estimates a longitudinally trained rate of physiological change. GrimAge has been the strongest mortality predictor in large comparisons described in the source, while DunedinPACE can be more responsive to weight loss because body-mass change contributed to its training target. Disagreement is therefore expected and is not resolved by choosing whichever score improved. (@FoundMyFitness (FoundMyFitness) — "The 7 Habits of People Who Age Slower | Dr. Steve Horvath", 2026-06-10, [link](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=3pRiY2zHt8c))

Clocks trained for different outcomes can disagree because they measure different feature sets and optimize different targets. Their research value is the possibility of detecting directional change over 3–12 months instead of waiting decades for disease or death, but that convenience creates a surrogate-endpoint problem: an intervention may move a clock without improving health. Conventional blood pressure, lipids, glucose, kidney function, fitness, and body composition remain directly interpretable risk measures; richer interpretation of routine data may add more preventive value than an unvalidated consumer age number. (@ABCNewsIndepth (ABC News In-depth) — "The health trends outpacing regulation and putting people at risk | Four Corners Documentary", 2026-07-20, [link](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=77TTDkR3nbI))

Predictive validity is not surrogate validity. A clock can predict mortality without proving that an intervention-induced change predicts an intervention-induced change in mortality. Horvath considers the convergence with beneficial interventions increasingly encouraging, but explicitly notes that methylation clocks have not met the regulatory standard for an accepted surrogate endpoint. Trials should report several prespecified clocks plus functional and clinical measures; principal-component versions can have technical repeatability on the order of months, making small personal changes difficult to distinguish from noise. (@FoundMyFitness (FoundMyFitness) — "The 7 Habits of People Who Age Slower | Dr. Steve Horvath", 2026-06-10, [link](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=3pRiY2zHt8c))

Intervention evidence follows baseline burden and endpoint choice. Antiretroviral treatment in people with HIV and anti-TNF treatment in inflammatory disease produce comparatively large clock improvements alongside treatment of major pathology. Caloric restriction produced a small DunedinPACE signal without GrimAge or PhenoAge changes in a trial with limited adherence; a semaglutide preprint in obesity reportedly moved multiple clocks after substantial weight loss. Omega-3, multivitamin, vitamin D, and exercise trials generally show smaller, clock-dependent changes measured in months, not proof of years of added life. The claim that small short-term clock changes compound linearly for decades is plausible speculation, not demonstrated longitudinal evidence. (@FoundMyFitness (FoundMyFitness) — "The 7 Habits of People Who Age Slower | Dr. Steve Horvath", 2026-06-10, [link](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=3pRiY2zHt8c))

Observational methylation associations support prioritizing smoking avoidance, healthy weight, vegetables, physical activity, and social connection, but they do not rank causal effect sizes. Horvath highlighted a strong vegetable-intake correlation and a newer association between cumulative social advantage and lower GrimAge; both can reflect correlated behaviors and social conditions. Reports of inherited effects from parental or grandparental stress remain substantially contested, whereas severe personal chronic stress has more support and ordinary short-term deadline stress appears weakly related to clock age. (@FoundMyFitness (FoundMyFitness) — "The 7 Habits of People Who Age Slower | Dr. Steve Horvath", 2026-06-10, [link](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=3pRiY2zHt8c))

A structural challenge to clock interpretation comes from stochastic clocks. Clocks constructed from random dispersion alone — accumulated, undirected methylation noise with no regulated program behind it — can account for roughly 70 to 80% of the prediction achieved by conventional clocks. This does not make clocks useless, since a measure of accumulated randomness is a legitimate proxy for elapsed biological wear. It does constrain what a clock reading means: most of the signal may be counting damage events rather than reporting on a regulated aging program that an intervention could plausibly reverse. Consistent with this, methylation data in humans decompose into a linear component with statistically independent contributing sites and a second, faster-accelerating component, suggesting a clock reading mixes at least two processes with different reversibility. [[aging-dynamics-and-resilience]] [[stochastic-aging-and-molecular-noise]] (@TheSheekeyScienceShow (The Sheekey Science Show) — "the 3 levels of aging therapeutics", 2026-02-08, [link](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=c-_Pdp5IIvw))

The blocking constraint on using clocks as clinical trial endpoints is validation rather than mechanism. There is currently no basis for claiming that a person predicted five years younger will live five years longer, because the longitudinal data that would license such a claim do not exist; what exists is a large body of association studies linking clock readings to mortality and disease, which establishes predictive power without establishing that an intervention-induced change carries the same meaning. Generating longitudinal data is the field's stated next step, and it is the step that would make clocks usable as surrogate endpoints — which would in turn accelerate everything downstream, since a trial could read out an intervention's effect at a time point rather than waiting for lifespan. Until then a clock is a research instrument, not a trial endpoint. (@TheSheekeyScienceShow (The Sheekey Science Show) — "How Randomness Drives Aging - DNA Repair, Clocks & Rejuvenation (David Meyer)", 2025-07-04, [link](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Buj07nWt7o0))

The endpoint problem should not obscure a second use in which validation matters much less. As **discovery tools**, clocks answer questions that are otherwise hard to pose at all — most usefully, whether different cell types within one organism age at the same rate. Applied to *C. elegans*, where individual cells are well defined and trackable, clocks show that they do not: some neuronal cell types age roughly twice as fast as others, against the naive expectation that every cell shares the organism's chronological age. The finding is validated by an independent consequence rather than by the clock itself. Neurons predicted older at a young adult stage are the ones that degenerate earlier, so a clock reading taken early carries genuine prognostic information about which cells will fail — which then makes it possible to ask what pathways distinguish them and to screen for compounds that counteract the difference. A clock used this way needs to be informative, not calibrated, which is why this application is available now while the endpoint application is not. (@TheSheekeyScienceShow (The Sheekey Science Show) — "How Randomness Drives Aging - DNA Repair, Clocks & Rejuvenation (David Meyer)", 2025-07-04, [link](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Buj07nWt7o0))

A stronger form of the same challenge comes from injecting noise deliberately. Adding random numbers to a young sample's data, repeatedly, moves conventional clocks approximately linearly with the amount added — so a clock reading substantially reports how far a sample has dispersed from a regulated reference rather than which pathways have moved. The corollary bears directly on how clock features are usually interpreted: developmental-pathway enrichment among clock CpG sites, long read as evidence that developmental programs keep running and drive aging, follows instead from fully repressed genes having the most room to drift and therefore dominating feature selection. Transcriptomic clocks show the same noise correlation, though less cleanly than methylation clocks. This is a stronger claim than the 70-to-80% dispersion figure above and arrives from a different analysis rather than replicating it. [[stochastic-aging-and-molecular-noise]] [[programmed-versus-stochastic-aging]] (@TheSheekeyScienceShow (The Sheekey Science Show) — "How Randomness Drives Aging - DNA Repair, Clocks & Rejuvenation (David Meyer)", 2025-07-04, [link](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Buj07nWt7o0))

Interventions that directly alter the measured compartment create a distinct interpretive hazard. A 2025 randomized trial of therapeutic plasma exchange in healthy adults reported an average 2.6-year reduction in biological age after about one month in its best-performing arm, assessed across DNA methylation, proteomics, and glycomics. Using several measurement families is a methodological strength, but the procedure replaces plasma proteins and, in that arm, infuses immunoglobulin — and plasma proteins and immunoglobulin glycosylation are direct inputs to proteomic and glycomic age estimates. Part of the measured change may therefore be the mechanical consequence of swapping the measured compartment rather than a change in tissue biology. [[therapeutic-plasma-exchange]] (@TheSheekeyScienceShow (The Sheekey Science Show) — "This years biggest breakthroughs in longevity! (2025)", 2025-12-21, [link](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=X-Hzyzo1Jpk))

Method dependence can be large enough to dominate the reported effect, which is easiest to see when one research line reports the same intervention twice. Concentrated pig-plasma fractions in old rats were reported as producing roughly 54% epigenetic rejuvenation in a 2020 preprint and, in later peer-reviewed work using a different method, 6 to 7% on average with higher values in some organs. Whatever the biology, a tenfold spread attributable to measurement choice means a percentage-rejuvenation figure describes a pipeline as much as an animal — which is why a replication of that work routes methylation measurement through the original laboratory's foundation to hold the method fixed, and adds grip strength, memory, blood chemistry, and survival endpoints that do not shift when the clock does. [[pig-plasma-fraction-rejuvenation]] [[plasma-derived-extracellular-particles]] (@TheSheekeyScienceShow (The Sheekey Science Show) — "Reproducing Rejuvenation: Inside the Pig Plasma Longevity Experiments", 2025-08-22, [link](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Q-lS1UMHG1o))

Mortality-risk scores are not necessarily biological-age measures. [[nmr-blood-analysis|MVX]] combines inflammatory and metabolic-malnutrition spectral features and reportedly predicts mortality with little association to chronological age, including in a described young-adult cohort. Its proponent, James Otvos, interprets it as metabolic vulnerability—the capacity to survive whatever disease or stress occurs—rather than the rate of acquiring a particular disease. This is a distinctive, observationally supported hypothesis; it is not evidence that MVX measures aging, causes death, or is a validated treatment target. (@PeterAttiaMD (Peter Attia MD) — "402 ‒ NMR blood analysis: how mortality risk and more can be assessed from a single blood sample", 2026-08-03, [link](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=IMbghqZ1iXI))

LinAge illustrates a second kind of relabeling: clinical chemistry, health-history, and lifestyle inputs estimate ten-year mortality risk and then express that risk as the chronological age with an equivalent model prediction. A lower age-equivalent score can be directionally coherent with improved ApoB, insulin-related markers, and body composition, but it is not a direct measurement of tissue age or proof that mortality risk actually changed by the displayed number of years. Kaeberlein considers LinAge 2 more clinically useful than many consumer clocks because its components and risk interpretation are more actionable; this is his expert preference, and the transcript provides one uncontrolled example rather than comparative validation. (@matt.kaeberlein (Healthspan Medicine) — "Optispan Success Story: What 18 Months of Real Biomarker Data Actually Looks Like with Carlos Pinto", 2026-03-01, [link](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=qlv8Q-CHWcc))

## Practical implications

Use established clinical risk factors at guideline-recommended intervals. If using a biological-age test, obtain the same laboratory platform and a prespecified panel including both state/risk and pace measures, repeat only after a meaningful intervention interval measured in months, and demand changes larger than technical error. Interpret results alongside function and conventional outcomes, and do not use a single score to justify supplements or unapproved treatments. Evidence for clocks as cohort and trial measures is moderate; evidence that consumer-guided clock optimization improves health is absent. ## Gaps & open questions

- Which clocks are causal surrogates rather than correlates?
- What change exceeds technical and day-to-day biological variation?
- Do score-guided interventions improve morbidity or mortality?
- How much of a blood-clock change is altered immune-cell composition rather than within-cell aging?
- Do small supplement-associated changes persist, accumulate, plateau, or disappear over decades?
- Does intervention-induced change in LinAge predict intervention-induced morbidity or mortality beyond the component clinical risk factors?
- If stochastic dispersion reproduces most of a clock's predictive power, what does the remaining regulated component measure, and is it the part an intervention could move?
- How should clock results be interpreted for interventions that directly replace or infuse the material being assayed?
- How much of a reported percentage rejuvenation is a property of the clock and analysis pipeline rather than of the treated animal, and should such figures be reported without a specified method at all?
- What longitudinal study design and duration would establish that an intervention-induced clock change predicts an intervention-induced survival change, and is any such study underway?
- Does cell-type-specific clock divergence exist in mammals as it does in *C. elegans*, and does it predict which cells degenerate first?
- If prediction error is the informative part of a first-generation clock, how much of that error is measurement noise rather than differential aging?
