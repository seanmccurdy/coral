# Gray-market peptides

The live disagreement: the wellness ecosystem promotes non-approved
peptides (via peptide clinics, compounding pharmacies, and
"research-use-only" vendors) as regenerative tools that succeed where
conventional medicine failed; skeptics led by [[peter-attia]] argue the
gray market is "the salvage yard for the drugs pharma tested and walked
away from," and that widespread use has run ahead of evidence that never
arrived (Peter Attia MD — "403 ‒ Peptides: separating scientific promise
from marketing hype", 2026-08-10,
[link](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Km_01CLEkYU)).

## Proponents' arguments and Attia's rebuttals

All from (Peter Attia MD — "403 ‒ Peptides: separating scientific promise
from marketing hype", 2026-08-10,
[link](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Km_01CLEkYU)):

- **"It worked for me / my friend."** Testimonials can't supply the
  counterfactual. Injuries regress to the mean (people start peptides at
  their worst), improvements coincide with rest, physical therapy, sleep,
  diet, training changes, and sometimes anabolic agents or GLP-1 drugs —
  yet the peptide gets the credit (his "Wolverine stack" anecdote: the
  dramatic before/after photos belonged to someone who had also started
  exercising, tirzepatide, a changed diet, and testosterone). Add the
  [[placebo-effect]] and reporting bias (responders post, non-responders
  move on).
- **"Pharma ignores peptides because natural molecules can't be
  patented."** Only partially true: raw products of nature can't be
  patented, but modified analogs, salts, conjugates, delivery systems,
  manufacturing processes, dosing regimens, and uses all can. Rapamycin,
  metformin, and the statins began as natural molecules; an entire
  industry is racing to build synthetic GLP-1 variants. If gray-market
  peptides delivered as claimed, pharma would be racing to develop them
  too — and many wellness peptides are in fact drugs pharma abandoned for
  inadequate efficacy, safety concerns, poor pharmacokinetics, or better
  competitors (see [[cjc-1295]] vs tesamorelin).
- **"Mine comes from a doctor / compounding pharmacy / third-party-tested
  vendor."** These reduce some risks but don't solve the fundamental
  problems: a prescription doesn't create missing evidence; a compounded
  version doesn't inherit the studied product's safety and efficacy;
  HPLC/mass-spec testing can confirm identity and purity of a sampled
  vial but says nothing about sterility or lot-to-lot consistency.
- **"It's the same molecule as the approved drug."** The molecule is not
  the drug. A pharmaceutical is the solved set of manufacturing,
  purification, formulation, and batch-consistency problems; clinical
  trials validate a specific product made under specific processes, not
  every preparation sharing an amino acid sequence (retatrutide example).

## What FDA approval buys (and costs)

Completing formal development yields demonstrated benefit in a defined
population, studied dose/formulation/route, characterized safety and
interactions, manufacturing standards, and post-market surveillance —
approval is indication-specific, not a blanket "safe" (SS-31 example:
approvable risk-benefit in Barth syndrome says nothing about healthy
people taking it for energy or longevity). Sticking to approved drugs
costs early access, cheaper options, and marginal-benefit compounds;
Attia judges the trade clearly worth it for a generally healthy person,
for whom the evidence bar should be higher, not lower (Peter Attia MD —
"403 ‒ Peptides: separating scientific promise from marketing hype",
2026-08-10, [link](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Km_01CLEkYU)).
