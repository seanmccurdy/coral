# CJC-1295

A growth-hormone-releasing-hormone (GHRH) analog and one of the peptides
[[peter-attia]] is asked about most. It is both biologically plausible
and biologically active — it can raise growth hormone and IGF-1 — which
places it in his bucket 2: real biology, but no demonstrated human
outcome benefit (Peter Attia MD — "403 ‒ Peptides: separating scientific
promise from marketing hype", 2026-08-10,
[link](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Km_01CLEkYU)).

## Why biological activity isn't enough

Almost every studied drug "changes biology"; the relevant question is
whether those changes translate into outcomes that matter — strength,
function, healing, quality of life, lifespan. The strongest argument
against CJC-1295 is that we already know what happens when the growth
hormone pathway is pushed more directly: in growth-hormone-replete
adults, GH itself produces modest body-composition changes (a meaningful
portion of "lean mass" gains reflecting water retention and other
non-contractile tissue) but benefits on strength, performance, recovery,
and quality of life that are generally much smaller than imagined and
often absent. That sets a high burden of proof for an indirect GHRH
analog to do dramatically better (Peter Attia MD — "403 ‒ Peptides:
separating scientific promise from marketing hype", 2026-08-10,
[link](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Km_01CLEkYU)). GH replacement can
matter in true deficiency and in specific conditions such as
HIV-associated lipodystrophy (Peter Attia MD — "403 ‒ Peptides:
separating scientific promise from marketing hype", 2026-08-10,
[link](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Km_01CLEkYU)).

## Development history: abandoned, and outcompeted by tesamorelin

CJC-1295 and tesamorelin share the same underlying biology and were
developed around the same time. CJC-1295 reached phase 2 and was
abandoned; tesamorelin — actually closer to the native molecule —
advanced to phase 3 and received full FDA approval because the data were
better, not because it was more patentable or more natural. CJC-1295 is
named after ConjuChem, the pharma company that abandoned it — an example
of the gray market as the "salvage yard" for drugs pharma tested and
walked away from (Peter Attia MD — "403 ‒ Peptides: separating scientific
promise from marketing hype", 2026-08-10,
[link](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Km_01CLEkYU)).
