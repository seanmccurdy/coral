# Dan Garner

Dan Garner is a human-performance coach (UFC fighters, Olympic and professional athletes, executives) whose distinguishing method is blood-analysis-driven constraint finding: treat the body like a system with a single binding bottleneck, locate it with labs and structured feedback, remove it, and let ordinary training and nutrition work. His formative observation, from functional-medicine work around 2014, was that resolving health problems (gut issues, migraines, deficiencies) improved body composition and training tolerance as a side effect — health itself was the plateau. He co-founded a blood-work company (Vitality) with Andy Galpin, a disclosed commercial interest adjacent to his testing-centric advice. ## Evidence stance and distinctive positions

Garner distinguishes scientific evidence (what works at the population level) from coaching evidence (what works for this individual under measurement), and runs a two-phase model: prescribe from population science first, then hand off to measure-and-adjust, with adherence explicitly outranking physiology. His signature frameworks in the corpus: constraint theory ("A constraint is a bottleneck toward progression in the result that you want"); the three-clock taxonomy of blood-marker reliability with reference change values ([[blood-marker-variability-and-reference-change]]); staged fat loss with a built-in maintenance break ([[low-energy-availability-and-menstrual-function]], [[energy-balance-and-calorie-counting]]); hypertrophy clusters and the one-bad-rep set-termination rule; and a deload executed as a 50%-volume preview week of the next training block rather than a scheduled recovery week of the current one ([[training-frequency-and-hypertrophy]]). (@drandygalpin (Andy Galpin) — "How to Reach Your Fitness Goals (Hard-to-Grow Muscles, Lose Fat, Busy Schedule & More) | Dan Garner", 2026-08-19, [link](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=-M50mes3nig))

Contrarian positions worth tracking against the wider corpus: a 20% testosterone change is inside measurement noise, and within-range testosterone differences matter little for muscle growth (credited to Ben House); vitamin D readings in inflamed or higher-body-fat people are partly artifact, so deficiency-megadose loops are often misinterpretation; dieters should bias calories toward the evening against standard sleep-hygiene advice, because a hungry, sympathetically activated dieter sleeps worse than a fed one; large caffeine doses before leg training reduce performance by inflating perceived exertion; creatine and beta-alanine are not pre-workout ingredients because neither is efficacious within the pre-workout window; and healthy trainees do not need four-week deload cycles. On gray-market compounds his stance is surveillance rather than endorsement or blindness: prescribing is malpractice, but so is ignoring a client who will use them anyway — measure, know the mechanism, keep them safe; his named warning archetype is the "charismatic mechanism guy" who sells pathways without population, dose, or comparator ([[experimental-peptides]]). ## Practical implications

- Use his material as elite-coaching practice with an unusually explicit measurement philosophy; most protocols (deficit staging, clusters, marker thresholds) are field-tested but not trial-tested, and several conflict with trial-anchored positions elsewhere in the wiki (omega-3 dose, evening eating).
- His testing-centric framing is adjacent to a commercial blood-analysis interest; weigh cadence recommendations (quarterly panels) accordingly.

## Gaps & open questions

- Which of his numeric thresholds (testosterone RCV 29%, ±150 ng/dL significance band, vitamin D 40 ng/mL injury threshold) hold up against primary laboratory-medicine sources?
- Would his cluster protocol and next-block deload outperform conventional programming in a controlled comparison?
