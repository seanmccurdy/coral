# Felice Jacka

Felice Jacka is a professor of nutritional psychiatry and director of the Food and Mood Centre at Deakin University in Australia, widely credited with helping create the field of nutritional psychiatry; her work has been cited in more than 100 policy documents for organizations including the World Health Organization. Her research arc runs from well-controlled cohort analyses establishing the diet-quality–depression association across countries and life stages, through the SMILES trial — the first randomized controlled trial of dietary improvement as a treatment for diagnosed major depression — to mechanistic microbiome work including cross-diagnostic microbial signatures of serious mental illness and a placebo-controlled fermented-dairy trial showing hippocampal volume and connectivity changes in healthy women. ## Evidence stance and distinctive perspectives

Her method emphasizes confounder discipline: she attributes the field's acceptance to datasets that carried not just good dietary and psychiatric measurement but income, education, body size, and health-behavior covariates, and she repeatedly pre-empts the body-weight explanation — the diet–depression link appears at every level of body mass index, and SMILES improved depression without weight change. Her distinctive positions include: whole grains over legumes as her current strongest single dietary correlate of good mental and physical health; emulsifier presence as a practical shorthand for identifying ultra-processed products worth avoiding; the position that nutritionally balanced ultra-processed food is still not equivalent to real food, supported by her group's meal-replacement trial in which equal weight loss produced opposite microbiome-diversity changes; and a structural rather than individual framing of dietary responsibility — naming the it's-their-own-fault framing as the field's most damaging myth and calling for food-environment change so that less healthy foods are not the default. She is restrained on supplements (weak, adjunctive evidence in mental disorders; she personally takes only wintertime vitamin D) and locates complexity honestly: the science is complicated, but feeding a microbiome is simple — varied plants, some fermented foods, few long-ingredient-list packets. ## Practical implications

- Use her material as expert research interpretation from a field founder; several of her group's key results (SMILES, the fermented-dairy imaging trial, the meal-replacement trial) are small or first-of-kind and await independent replication.
- Note affiliations when weighing specific studies: the fermented-dairy trial was run with a commercial dairy partner, and her appearances on the ZOE platform sit alongside ZOE's commercial fermented-food and gut-health products.

## Gaps & open questions

- Which of her group's foundational results have been independently replicated, and at what effect sizes?
- How does her whole-grains-first ranking hold up against legume, fermented-food, and diversity-focused comparisons in interventional designs?
